### Slide 3


Alright, you've seen what Elevate Zen does.
But now, let's take a look at what's actually happening behind the scenes.
Because for Elevate Zen to work effectively, we need to handle something very important:
Patient information doesn't always come in one single format.
A patient might explain their symptoms by speaking.
They might type out some information.
And they might also have previous medical reports, prescriptions or other documents that need to be considered.
So our system needs to be able to take all of these different forms of information and bring them together.
And this is where our technical approach comes in.
STEP 1 — INPUT
The patient first provides their information through our interface.
This can happen through speech, text, or uploaded medical documents.
So whether the patient is describing their symptoms verbally or providing an existing medical report, the system has a way of bringing that information into the case-taking process.

STEP 2 — EXTRACTION
Now, let's say the patient chooses to speak.
We use Automatic Speech Recognition, or ASR, to convert the patient's speech into text.
This gives our system something that can then be processed and analyzed.
Now, what happens if the patient uploads an old medical report?
That's where OCR — Optical Character Recognition — comes in.
OCR allows us to extract information from the uploaded document so that the contents of that report can also become part of the patient's digital history.

So now we're able to take information from two very different sources:
Speech and Documents and bring them into the same digital workflow.

STEP 3 — UNDERSTANDING THE INFORMATION

But simply converting everything into text isn't enough.
Imagine we have a huge paragraph containing the patient's symptoms, their previous medical history and information from an old report.
A doctor doesn't necessarily want to read through one giant block of automatically generated text.
They need the important information to be identified and organized.
This is where we use language models and Clinical NLP.
The system processes the extracted information and identifies relevant clinical details — such as:
What are the patient's current symptoms?
What is their previous medical history?
What information is present in their test results?

Instead of leaving everything as unstructured text, the information is organized into meaningful clinical categories.

So we're essentially transforming:

Raw information → Structured clinical information.

STEP 4 — PATIENT HISTORY AND TIMELINE

Once that information has been organized, we can assemble it into a structured patient history.
And this is where the timeline becomes useful.
Instead of having previous records scattered across different documents, the relevant information can be organized chronologically.

For example:

Previous medical history → Previous test → Earlier consultation → Current symptoms.

This gives the doctor a much clearer overview of the patient's journey before the consultation even begins.
And that's the real purpose of all these technologies.
We're not using AI just because it sounds impressive.

Each technology has a specific role:

ASR helps us capture spoken information.

OCR helps us extract information from documents.

Language models and Clinical NLP help us understand and organize that information.
STEP 5 — DOCTOR VERIFICATION

But there's one final and extremely important stage.
The doctor.
The information generated by the AI is not automatically treated as the final medical record.
The doctor gets the structured patient profile and can review it, edit it, and verify it before submission.
This is especially important in healthcare because AI can make mistakes.

For example, the system could misunderstand something that a patient said or incorrectly extract information from a document. So instead of allowing the AI to make the final decision, we keep the doctor in the loop.
And this brings us to one of the most important principles behind Elevate Zen:
The AI is not here to replace the doctor.
The AI's job is to take scattered patient information and turn it into structured information that the doctor can quickly understand, review and verify.
The clinical decision still belongs to the doctor.

### Slide 4

Now, because this involves healthcare, we didn't just ask ourselves whether the system can work.
We also asked a much more important question:
Where can it fail?
Because when we're dealing with something as sensitive as a patient's medical history, an error isn't just a technical inconvenience.
It could potentially affect the way that information is understood by the doctor.

So we identified some of the major challenges that our system could face, and more importantly, how we plan to address them.
First — Incorrect AI summaries.
Our system uses AI to organize patient information.
But what happens if the AI misunderstands something the patient says?
For example, if a symptom is misunderstood or an important detail is incorrectly summarized, the resulting patient profile could give an inaccurate picture of the patient's history.

So we don't want to blindly trust the AI output.
Our approach includes clinical question flows, validation rules, and mandatory doctor verification.
In other words, the AI prepares the information but the doctor verifies it.

The generated information is treated as an assistive draft, not as an unquestionable medical truth.

Second — Speech recognition in real-world environments.
A hospital isn't exactly a quiet recording studio. There could be conversations happening around the patient, equipment sounds, people moving around, or simply different accents and languages.
So speech recognition can become less accurate in noisy environments. To address this, our approach includes multilingual ASR, audio prompts, touch-based alternatives, and confirmation steps. So if speech isn't the most reliable option at a particular moment, the system doesn't force the patient to depend entirely on speech. The patient can confirm or provide information through another interaction method.This gives us a more flexible and accessible case-taking process.

Third — Privacy and sensitive medical information.

And this is probably one of the most important challenges. Medical records contain extremely sensitive information.
Our system may be dealing with patient conversations, medical history, test reports and other personal health information. So privacy can't be something we think about after building the application. It has to be considered as part of the system itself.
That's why our approach includes measures such as OCR confidence checks, patient correction, secure encryption and consent controls.

The idea is simple:
The more sensitive the information, the more carefully we need to handle it. So our focus isn't only on making the AI intelligent.
We're also thinking about making the overall system reliable, controllable and responsible.
